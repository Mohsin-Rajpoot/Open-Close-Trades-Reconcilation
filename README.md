# Open-Close-Trades-Reconcilation

A Python-based trade reconciliation system that automates the matching and aggregation of options trades from brokerage data, calculating accurate profit and loss across thousands of positions.
##Overview
This project addresses a real accounting problem that options traders face. When you place a large options order, brokers typically split it into multiple smaller fills executed at different times and prices. A single position might be opened through five separate transactions on different days, each at slightly different prices. Manually tracking which fills belong together, calculating weighted average entry prices, matching closing transactions back to opens, and computing accurate P&L takes considerable time and introduces errors.
The system processes 6,786 raw trade records from broker exports and produces 949 clean, reconciled positions with complete entry and exit data. It automatically pairs option legs to construct spreads, aggregates multiple fills using weighted average pricing, matches closing transactions to their corresponding opens, and calculates realized profit and loss for each completed trade.
##The Problem
Consider a typical scenario. You decide to open a bull put spread on a stock, selling 30 contracts of a higher strike put and buying 30 contracts of a lower strike put to cap your risk. Your broker executes this order in three separate fills: 10 contracts at one price, 5 at another, and 15 at yet another. In your CSV export, this appears as six rows of data: three for the short leg and three for the long leg.
Later you close this position, again in multiple fills. Now you have additional rows in your data representing the closing transactions. To properly track this trade, you need to recognize that these dozen or so rows all represent a single position. You need to calculate the weighted average entry price, match the closes to the opens, and compute the realized gain or loss.
Do this manually for a few positions and it's tedious. Do it for hundreds of trades spanning years and it becomes impractical. This system automates the entire workflow.
##Technical Approach
The reconciliation follows a multi-stage process. First, the system parses CSV data exported from the broker. The data often uses forward-filled formatting where trade details appear on the first row and subsequent rows contain only the changing values. The parser handles this by propagating values down and converting dates to standard formats.
The next stage groups trades into logical units. Trades are grouped by execution date, symbol, expiration date, option type, and spread type. Within each group, the system identifies which rows represent option legs that belong together.
For vertical spreads, the pairing logic matches sell legs with buy legs. If a group contains three sell rows and three buy rows, the system pairs them in order: first sell with first buy, second sell with second buy, and so on. This creates three paired records, each showing both strikes and the combined net premium.
The aggregation phase is where multiple fills combine into single positions. The system groups these paired records by their defining characteristics: symbol, expiration, option type, and both strike prices. Notice it groups across all dates. If you opened the same spread on three different days, the aggregation recognizes this as the same position and combines all the fills. It sums the total contracts and calculates the weighted average price.
For the example above with fills of 10, 5, and 15 contracts at different prices, the weighted average calculation multiplies each quantity by its price, adds these products together, and divides by the total quantity. This produces the correct average cost basis.
The system applies this same aggregation logic to closing transactions. Multiple closing fills get combined into a single close record before being matched back to the open position. This ensures accurate P&L calculation even when positions are closed gradually over time.
Results
Processing 6,786 raw trade records produced 949 final positions, an 86 percent reduction in data volume. The reduction comes from several sources. Pairing option legs combines two rows into one. Aggregating multiple fills reduces several rows to a single position. Filtering irrelevant data removes noise.
The vertical spread aggregation specifically reduced 1,186 individual fill records to 641 positions, a 46 percent reduction. The closing transaction aggregation reduced 1,036 individual fills to 457 closing records, a 56 percent reduction. These numbers demonstrate how effectively the system handles real-world data where positions are built and closed through multiple transactions.
The financial reconciliation calculated 3.3 million dollars in realized profit and loss across 653 closed positions. Another 296 positions were classified as expired because they passed their expiration date without a recorded closing transaction in the data. These likely expired worthless or were exercised.
The processing time for the complete dataset averaged 4 seconds. Manual reconciliation of the same data would require approximately 20 hours, representing a 95 percent time reduction.
##Implementation Details
The code is organized into distinct stages. The parsing stage loads the CSV, handles forward-filled values, normalizes dates, and calculates signed premiums. Sell transactions get positive premiums and buy transactions get negative premiums, making the math cleaner.
The matching stage implements the leg pairing algorithm. It uses pandas groupby operations to organize trades into logical groups, then pairs legs within each group. The code preserves execution order when pairing to maintain data integrity.
The aggregation stage groups matched pairs by position-defining characteristics and applies sum and weighted average calculations. This uses pandas' powerful aggregation functions with custom logic for the price averaging.
The close matching stage works differently for single options versus spreads. For naked options, it matches the opposite side: a short position closes with a buy, a long position closes with a sell. For vertical spreads, both strikes must match simultaneously. The code tracks which closes have been consumed to prevent double-matching.
The P&L calculation stage applies different formulas depending on spread type. Credit spreads use the formula: entry credit minus closing debit equals profit. Debit spreads use: closing credit minus entry debit equals profit. The code determines which formula to apply based on whether the entry premium was positive or negative.
The output stage formats the reconciled data into Excel workbooks with multiple analytical views. It generates the main reconciliation file, a focused view of closed trades, and a comprehensive analysis workbook with 15 different sheets examining performance from various angles.
##Accounting Principles Applied
The system demonstrates understanding of proper options accounting. Strike relationships determine strategy classification. For put spreads, if the short strike is higher than the long strike, it's a bull put spread. The reverse relationship indicates a bear put spread. Call spreads follow similar but opposite logic.
Cost basis calculations follow weighted average methodology as required for proper accounting. When you build a position over time at different prices, you can't simply average the prices. You must weight each price by the quantity purchased at that price. The system implements this correctly.
The treatment of debits and credits follows standard accounting practices. Credits represent money received and are recorded as positive. Debits represent money paid and are recorded as negative. The P&L calculation properly accounts for these flows.
Position reconciliation maintains an audit trail from raw data through final output. Each aggregated position can be traced back to its component fills. Each matched close links to its corresponding open. This provides the documentation needed for auditing and verification.

##Challenges and Solutions
Several challenges emerged during development. The first involved recognizing when multiple rows represented the same position. The solution groups by position-defining characteristics: symbol, expiration, option type, and both strikes for spreads. This grouping works across different dates, properly identifying positions built over time.
Another challenge was distinguishing disguised spreads from true naked options. Sometimes a single order type contains both a buy and a sell at different strikes, making it actually a spread. Other times, it's genuinely a naked position. The solution analyzes the combination of sides and strikes before classifying the position.
Partial closes presented difficulties. A position opened with 30 contracts might be closed in three separate transactions of 10, 12, and 8 contracts. The system needed to aggregate these closes before calculating P&L. The solution applies the same aggregation logic to closes as it does to opens.
Data quality issues required careful handling. Naked options legitimately have missing values for one strike. Expired positions lack closing data. The system distinguishes between expected gaps and data problems, handling the former appropriately while flagging the latter.
